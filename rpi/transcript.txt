========================================================================

This recording is one training programme for all Learning Hive tutors and relevant staff. You will hear eleven modules. Have the current policy available. Welcome to Learning Hive’s training on de-escalating behaviour, reasonable force and restrictive physical intervention awareness. This is for every tutor and relevant member of staff, including online tutors. Our aim is to keep learners and adults safe through thoughtful planning, appropriate support and clear escalation. We want you to be able to recognise when the current arrangement is no longer safe and act early, rather than struggle through a session to meet its scheduled finish time.

The abbreviation R P I stands for restrictive physical intervention. You also need to recognise restrictions without touch. If you prevent someone moving, the action may be restraint even if your hands never touch them. A familiar example is standing in a doorway so a learner cannot leave. A voluntary break is different: the learner chooses the space and remains free to leave it.

Reasonable force means using no more force than is needed, and only to prevent immediate harm when there is no safer option. Any use of force must have a lawful basis and be necessary and proportionate to the immediate circumstances. A qualification, a completed course or a parent’s permission does not change that, and this training does not give you any additional power.

This training covers awareness, de-escalation and how to follow each pupil’s plan. It does not teach physical holds, and passing it does not make you qualified to restrain a child. Your role is to know the plan, use its preventative strategies, recognise danger and call for help. You must not improvise a restraint technique.

Before you start with any pupil, Learning Hive will send you their risk assessment. Every risk assessment has a Red, Amber or Green rating, which shows the level of risk. Learning Hive writes the risk assessment and sets the rating. Your role is to read it, ask about anything you do not understand, and sign to confirm you have understood it before tuition starts. Module two explains what each rating means for you.

Before you work unsupervised, you must read the policy, watch this training, pass the online assessment, demonstrate a safe response in your scenario assessment and sign the declaration, which is part of the online assessment. A manager then signs off your completion. Ask about unclear instructions before starting; a training completion tick cannot replace that conversation.

Before your first session, locate the verified Learning Hive contact information provided with your induction. Save the live safeguarding contact, management contact and backup route where you can reach them. Do not rely on an untested office number to provide emergency cover.

MODULE 2: KNOW THE PUPIL AND PLAN FOR PREVENTION   [starts 4:11 in the full video · length 3:44]
========================================================================

Prevention starts before the first lesson. Read the risk assessment and support plan that Learning Hive sends you. Look for what has happened before, what the learner was experiencing, what helped and what made matters worse. A broad label such as “challenging behaviour” tells you very little. If the information you need is not there, ask Learning Hive.

A learner who has experienced trauma may interpret a raised voice, sudden movement or an adult standing over them as danger. A learner with communication differences may need extra processing time or a non-verbal way to ask for a break. Noise, light, pain, hunger, fatigue, unfamiliar adults and unexpected transitions may all affect engagement. These are reasons to understand and adjust the session, not reasons to assume the learner needs restraint. A diagnosis on its own does not mean a learner is high risk.

Every risk assessment has a Red, Amber or Green rating. Learning Hive sets the rating. You do not need to score it yourself, but you do need to understand what it means. Green means the risk is low and can be managed with the usual arrangements. Amber means there is a known, increased risk. Amber placements have a detailed risk assessment, extra safeguards and approval from the designated safeguarding lead, and you must follow that plan closely. Red means a high level of risk, such as a history of restraint, serious assault, weapons or dangerous running away. Red placements do not go ahead without review by the Director and the designated safeguarding lead, and are usually declined where the risks cannot be managed safely.

If the risk assessment mentions an incident but leaves out the details, or something important is missing, ask Learning Hive before the first session. If the plan relies on a parent being nearby, check what that means in practice, what they will do if you need them and whether they will actually be there. If anything changes, tell Learning Hive before the next session.

Before tuition starts, Learning Hive will talk you through the significant risks, the emergency procedures and when a session must stop. You then sign to confirm you have read and understood the risk assessment, and that confirmation is kept on the pupil’s file. This applies to every placement, including online tuition. Start with an achievable, predictable session. Explain what will happen, offer appropriate choice, agree how breaks work and notice early signs of distress.

MODULE 3: RECOGNISE ESCALATION AND RESPOND EARLY   [starts 7:55 in the full video · length 3:07]
========================================================================

Escalation does not follow one fixed pattern. Some learners raise their voice or pace; others stop talking, freeze, pull away or repeatedly ask to leave. Silence is not always calm. Learn this pupil’s signs rather than waiting for behaviour that looks dramatic. Ask yourself what has changed and whether the learning task, environment or adult response is increasing distress.

When early signs appear, reduce pressure. Stop adding questions. Use a quieter, steady voice and a short statement such as, “We can pause. You can sit here or use the agreed quiet space.” Allow time for the learner to respond. Do not keep repeating the same demand more loudly. Remove unnecessary audience or stimulation where safe, and use the pupil’s familiar regulation strategies without insisting that they must work immediately.

A relational approach means preserving trust while maintaining safety. You can acknowledge feelings without approving harm: “I can see this is hard. I’m moving back so we both have space.” Avoid arguing about blame, telling the learner to calm down, threatening punishment or requiring an apology during crisis. Keep your words predictable. The aim at that point is safety and regulation, not proving that an adult instruction will be obeyed.

Watch what your response does. If it increases distress, change it. A favourite distraction may help one learner but feel dismissive to another. An offered break may help, but it stops being a voluntary break if you then prevent departure. Choices should be real and safe; do not offer a choice that the pupil’s plan or the venue cannot support.

If risk continues to rise, switch from teaching to the crisis plan. Stop the task, create safe space and summon the agreed support. You do not have to use every de-escalation strategy before calling for emergency help when danger is immediate. Make that decision from the actual situation. Record new warning signs and what worked so the next plan can be more effective.

Take a moment to apply this module. A learner pushes their worksheet away and stops answering. An adult says, “Sit down and finish this now or I will call your mum.” How could you reduce pressure?

Pause the recording now and think through your answer. When you are ready, resume for the suggested response.

Here is the suggested response. One possible response is, “We can pause. Would you like a break here or in the agreed quiet space?” Put the work aside, use a calm voice and allow processing time. Use only choices the plan and venue can support. Watch whether the response helps. Do not keep repeating demands or insist that a regulation strategy must work immediately.

MODULE 4: BODY LANGUAGE, SPACE AND EXITS   [starts 11:02 in the full video · length 3:10]
========================================================================

Your position in the room matters as much as your words. Before the session, look at exits, furniture, visibility, public access and any hazard. Avoid arrangements where you must squeeze past the learner to leave or where the learner has to squeeze past you. Keep a phone accessible and check you can reach the parent or carer. A paper risk assessment cannot make a blocked doorway safe.

During escalation, avoid looming over the learner, pointing, sudden movements or stepping closer to demand an answer. Give space appropriate to the room and the risk. You may stand where you have a route to withdraw, but positioning near an exit does not mean occupying it so the learner cannot leave. The tutor and pupil both need safe movement routes. Do not use furniture, a door, your body or a threat to contain the learner.

Imagine a small library room. The pupil is distressed, and the tutor stands in the doorway saying the session is not finished. Even with no touch, that may restrict movement. Moving aside and phoning the parent or carer is a different action. Before a session in a public place, check how you will reach the parent or carer, that your phone has signal there, and how quickly they can come. Do not assume library staff are trained to help or have agreed to supervise the pupil after you leave.

A home session requires the same care. A parent upstairs is not automatically available immediately and is not automatically competent to assist with restraint. Confirm a way to summon them, who takes responsibility if the session stops, and what happens if they do not respond. If the plan cannot be delivered, contact Learning Hive; do not simply adapt it by using force.

Safe withdrawal means getting to a safer position and obtaining help while considering the learner’s welfare. It does not mean abandoning a child. Maintain safe oversight where feasible, contact the parent or carer or emergency services, and arrange handover. If there is immediate danger, your first action may be moving to safety and calling nine nine nine. Do not delay an emergency call while seeking permission from Learning Hive.

Keep furniture away from the exit and avoid a position that corners the learner or requires either person to squeeze past the other. During distress, create space and move away from the doorway. Know how you will contact the parent or carer. Being near an exit for your own safety must not become blocking the learner’s route.

MODULE 5: WHEN TO STOP A SESSION AND SUMMON HELP   [starts 14:13 in the full video · length 3:11]
========================================================================

A session can become unsafe even without a physical incident. Stop criteria may include escalating threats, unsafe movement, a weapon, a failed communication route, an absent adult required by the plan, or a change that makes the room unsuitable. Agree those criteria before the session. You should not improvise a new arrangement to keep delivery hours going when an essential control has failed.

If you can safely do so, stop the activity and explain simply: “We are pausing now so we can keep everyone safe.” Contact the parent or carer and make a safe handover to them. In a public place such as a library, do not assume a member of the public will help. Do not leave the child without a responsible arrangement. If risk is immediate, move to safety and call emergency services without waiting for that planned handover.

Call nine nine nine when there is an immediate threat to life or serious injury, a weapon posing immediate danger, or an urgent medical emergency. Give the precise location, what is happening, the person at risk, injuries and any access information. Follow the call handler’s directions. Do not try to disarm someone. For non-immediate concerns, use the agreed Learning Hive and safeguarding routes; if you are unsure about a developing emergency, explain the facts rather than trying to diagnose it yourself.

Contact Learning Hive as soon as it is safe. A call to a manager is not a substitute for a nine nine nine call in an emergency, and a form submission is not a way to summon immediate help. Know the live designated safeguarding lead or deputy and management cover contacts before delivery. If your first internal contact does not answer, use the agreed backup; do not allow an unanswered call to halt urgent safeguarding action.

Stopping a session for safety is your decision to make in the moment; it is not a punishment for the learner. Record the reason for the early stop, the handover and the actual attendance. Learning Hive decides what happens next, including any pause or changes to support. If the incident suggests a learner may need to be held in future, Learning Hive must review the arrangements before the next session goes ahead.

MODULE 6: RESPONDING WHEN A LEARNER LEAVES   [starts 17:24 in the full video · length 3:13]
========================================================================

“Absconding” describes a learner leaving unexpectedly, but the response depends on the context. A pupil asking for an agreed break is different from a young child running toward a road. Know the specific leaving risk, safe boundaries, adult roles and emergency thresholds in the plan. Do not make a routine decision to restrict movement simply because the pupil should be in a lesson.

Try calm, brief prompts and an agreed safe alternative when there is time. For example, “Let’s use the agreed break space,” may be appropriate for a pupil who understands that arrangement. Do not stand across the doorway or threaten punishment for leaving. If the learner leaves, keep them in sight where it is safe and possible, contact the parent or carer and Learning Hive, and give the last known location, direction and time.

Do not create a further risk through chasing into traffic, physical confrontation or leaving other children unsupervised. You must consider your own safety and the welfare of everyone for whom you have responsibility. If the learner is in immediate danger, call nine nine nine and describe that danger. If their whereabouts are unknown, follow the missing-child procedure and escalate promptly according to the risk; do not wait until the next session. Record the sequence of events, calls and outcomes.

An imminent injury may require an immediate lawful and proportionate protective response when there is no effective safer alternative. That is a judgement about the actual emergency, not permission to improvise a restraint hold. Once the danger ends, there is no justification to continue a restriction just to return the pupil to the table or finish the task.

A serious departure or a near miss is important information. The lesson should not simply restart next time unchanged. Report it so Learning Hive can review the plan, and wait to be briefed on any changes before the next session. A promise that the tutor will “watch more closely” is not enough if the same arrangement remains unsafe.

MODULE 7: MANAGING ESCALATION IN AN ONLINE SESSION   [starts 20:38 in the full video · length 3:19]
========================================================================

Online tutors also need this training. You cannot physically intervene through Zoom, but you may see or hear a serious welfare concern. A pupil can become distressed, disclose harm, threaten self-harm, leave the room or disconnect during an unsafe situation. The absence of face-to-face contact does not make the case automatically Green.

Before delivery, know the learner’s current address or physical location, the agreed emergency contact, the responsible adult where relevant, and the Learning Hive safeguarding cover. If the location can change between sessions, confirm the arrangement rather than relying on an old address. Check the plan says what happens if the connection ends or an adult cannot be reached. Respect privacy and use approved platforms and communication routes.

During distress, reduce demands and speak in calm, brief sentences. Ask for the agreed adult’s help where appropriate and keep contact if it is safe and useful. Do not argue, threaten or demand that the camera remain on as a condition of receiving help. Do not promise secrecy when a safeguarding concern is disclosed. Record the pupil’s words accurately and use the designated safeguarding lead route.

If an immediate threat to life or serious injury is apparent, call nine nine nine with the learner’s physical location even though you are elsewhere. State that you are the remote tutor and describe what you saw or heard. If you have incomplete location information, give what you know and act to resolve it; uncertainty is not a reason to ignore an emergency.

If the connection ends after a serious concern, act on the concern. Attempt the agreed contact routes, notify the designated safeguarding lead promptly and involve emergency services where immediate danger is suspected. Do not treat it as an ordinary attendance absence, send one message and wait for tomorrow. If the connection drops for a routine technical reason and there is no welfare concern, follow the usual escalation process. Record the reason for your escalation, with calls, times and outcomes. Learning Hive will review whether the plan and emergency contact arrangements are still adequate.

MODULE 8: RESTRICTIONS, EMERGENCY JUDGEMENT AND PROHIBITED PRACTICES   [starts 23:57 in the full video · length 3:08]
========================================================================

Recognise the action by its effect, not its friendly name. A “supportive hold” that prevents movement is still a restriction. A “calm room” becomes seclusion if the learner cannot leave. Taking away a walking aid to control movement is a restriction without touch. Report these actions even if no injury is visible and even if someone says the plan allowed them.

Learning Hive does not plan seclusion as part of its provision. Do not lock a learner in, barricade a room, threaten confinement or use your body to stop an ordinary departure. A voluntary break remains voluntary. You can set safe expectations and call for help without using confinement. Ordinary appropriate first aid or an accepted guiding hand is a different kind of contact, but consider the learner’s wishes, needs and safeguarding plan.

Force must never be punishment. Do not use it to make someone complete work, enforce a timetable, obtain an apology or protect property alone without an immediate proportionate safety justification. If there is imminent injury and no effective safer option, any protective action must have a lawful basis and be necessary and proportionate to the actual danger. Stop a restriction as soon as the danger ends. A plan, a training certificate and parental consent do not remove those conditions.

Learning Hive prohibits practices that affect airway, breathing or circulation; pressure to the neck, chest or abdomen; covering the mouth or nose; and prone, face-down or ground holds. It also prohibits mechanical or chemical restraint and forced searching under this policy. We do not demonstrate holds here or suggest an alternative hold you should try.

Remember the instruction: “If the situation goes beyond this plan, prioritise safety, call for help and contact Learning Hive. Do not improvise a restraint technique.” If you think a learner may need to be physically held to keep people safe, tell Learning Hive straight away. Lone tuition must not continue where that need is expected, and completing a training course does not change that.

Take a moment to apply this module. A colleague says that a learner was only kept in the room for a short time, with no physical contact and no visible injury. Does that mean no restrictive incident occurred?

Here is the suggested response. No. Preventing departure can restrict movement even without touch. A short duration or absence of a visible mark does not remove the need to report. Record the actual action, the immediate circumstances, how long it lasted and the learner’s response. Do not use a friendly name for an action to conceal its effect.

MODULE 9: AFTER AN INCIDENT AND THE FORMAL RECORD   [starts 27:05 in the full video · length 4:04]
========================================================================

After an incident, make sure the immediate danger has passed and check everyone’s welfare. Look for injury, breathing difficulty, distress and the need for first aid or medical assessment. Lack of a visible mark is not proof that no harm occurred. Give the learner calm support and time to recover. A debrief should not become an interrogation or a demand to agree with the adult’s account.

Tell the designated safeguarding lead or deputy and your Learning Hive contact as soon as safely possible. Use the live contact route for an urgent concern, then submit the formal safeguarding or incident form. The designated safeguarding lead ensures the appropriate My Concern record, safeguarding log and commissioner record. A WhatsApp attendance group is not the formal record and is not a suitable place for detailed sensitive incident accounts.

Record every actual or attempted force, restraint, seclusion, restriction of exit, injury and relevant near miss. Write as soon as practicable and aim to complete the account the same day. State who was there, time, place, approximate duration, relevant needs and what happened before the event. Describe the early signs, strategies attempted, immediate risk, exact adult actions, contact or force, learner response and when it stopped. Include injuries, medical support, witness accounts, notifications and departures from the plan.

Use observations and attributed words. “The pupil pushed the chair toward the doorway” is clearer than “The pupil was out of control.” Distinguish what you saw from what you inferred. Include the learner’s account in an accessible way and record disagreement without trying to resolve it yourself. The designated safeguarding lead coordinates family and commissioning-school or local authority notification, normally the same day, and written follow-up. If telling a particular parent might cause serious harm, escalate that question to the designated safeguarding lead rather than withholding the whole report.

All incidents involving restrictive physical intervention must be reported to the relevant local authority or commissioning body without delay, in accordance with their reporting procedures. Reports should include the learner’s risk assessment, R P I incident record, relevant staff training log and Behaviour and Relationships Policy, where required by the commissioner. The Learning Hive designated safeguarding lead will coordinate reporting and ensure records are maintained.

Learning Hive will review what happened and whether the next session is safe, and may ask you to take part in a debrief. If an adult may have used inappropriate force or concealed an incident, use the safeguarding allegation route. Staff and witnesses need support as well. You will be briefed on any changes to the plan before your next session.

MODULE 10: REVIEW THE RISK ASSESSMENT BEFORE YOU START   [starts 31:10 in the full video · length 4:25]
========================================================================

Learning Hive writes every pupil’s risk assessment and sets its Red, Amber or Green rating. You do not score or categorise the risk yourself. Your job is to read the risk assessment and support plan carefully, make sure you understand them, raise anything that is missing or does not match what you see, and sign to confirm before tuition starts.

A usable plan links each warning sign to something you should do. Look for the learner’s triggers and early signs, the strategies that help, the actions to avoid, their communication needs, how breaks work, and any sensory or health needs. If the plan only says something broad, such as “use de-escalation”, ask Learning Hive what that means for this learner.

Next, check the crisis arrangements. The plan should tell you when the session must stop, how to call for help, who the parent or carer is and how to reach them, when to call nine nine nine, and who takes responsibility for the learner if tuition ends early. Make sure you know the live Learning Hive safeguarding contacts before the first session.

Then check that the arrangements will work in practice. In a home, “parent upstairs” does not tell you whether they will hear you or come quickly. Agree how you will call them and what they will do. In a library or other public place, check you can reach the parent or carer, that your phone has signal, and how quickly they can get to you. Look at the room: where you will sit, the learner’s route out, and any hazards. Do not position yourself by an exit in a way that traps the learner.

For online sessions, check you know the learner’s physical location, the agreed emergency adult, and what to do if the connection ends. If the location can change, confirm it at the start of each session.

Before tuition starts, Learning Hive will discuss the significant risks, the emergency procedures and the stopping criteria with you. Use that conversation to ask questions. You will then sign a declaration confirming that you have read and understood the risk assessment and support plan, including the risks, preventative strategies, emergency procedures and safeguarding contacts. You also confirm that you understand when tuition must stop, and that you will tell Learning Hive immediately if circumstances change or the agreed safety arrangements cannot be maintained.

If something in the plan is missing, unclear or does not match what you find, tell Learning Hive before you start. If an arrangement the plan relies on is not in place on the day, do not carry on as normal. Contact the parent or carer and Learning Hive and follow their instructions. Never fill a gap by blocking a doorway or by using force.

If you ever think a learner may need to be physically held to keep people safe, tell Learning Hive straight away. Lone tuition must not continue where that need is expected. After any incident or near miss, the plan will be reviewed and you will be briefed on the changes before your next session.

Here is a worked example. A home tuition plan says the parent will be “nearby” and that the tutor should withdraw if behaviour escalates. It does not say how to reach the parent or who supervises the learner once the tutor has stepped away. These are gaps. Before the first session, ask Learning Hive to confirm how the parent will be contacted, that they will be available, and how the handover will work. Do not start until those answers are clear.
